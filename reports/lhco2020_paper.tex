% ==================== ABSTRACT ===================================
\begin{flushleft}
\sffamily\small
\textbf{Model-agnostic searches for new physics at the Large Hadron Collider rely on anomaly
detection, yet trade-offs between supervision, training contamination and invariant-mass
distortion remain poorly quantified. I benchmark unsupervised, weakly supervised and
supervised detectors on the LHC Olympics 2020 R\&D dijet dataset using five jet-substructure
features. Isolation Forest is the strongest learned unsupervised detector ($\auc = \IFHeadline$);
Deep SAD with 100 labelled anomalies reaches $\auc = \SADHeadline$, against $\SupMean$ for
the supervised reference, with $\jsd = \SADJSD$ and a descriptive local fixed-window diagnostic. Deep SAD retains $\auc = \SADTransferHeadline$ on held-out
three-prong events. Follow-up extensions test a masked Deep Sets autoencoder on constituent
inputs, two external signals, conformal calibration of an ANODE score and background-only
closure. The Deep Sets AUCs are 0.498 (nominal two-prong), 0.441 (three-prong), 0.465
($X\to YY'$) and 0.507 ($W_{KK}\to WR\to3W$). Sideband conformal $p$-values are consistent with uniformity (KS $= 0.0043$,
$p = 0.179$); background-only closure shows no local $Z \geq 3$ fluctuations for two of
three tested scores at a nominal 1\% cut. The richer representation did not improve
discrimination, and calibration and closure claims remain conditional on simulated
background.}
\end{flushleft}

\section{Introduction}

The discovery of physics beyond the Standard Model (BSM) at the Large
Hadron Collider (LHC) typically relies on targeted searches optimised
for a specific signal hypothesis. Model-agnostic \emph{anomaly
detection} approaches instead aim to identify rare, unusual events
against the dominant quantum chromodynamics (QCD) background, without assuming a
particular new-physics model.\cite{Karagiorgi2022,Belis2024} They are motivated by the
possibility that the full LHC dataset contains signatures that existing
analyses have overlooked. Such strategies have also moved from proposal to
practice: ATLAS and CMS have reported dijet and related resonance searches based on
weakly supervised, unsupervised and semi-supervised anomaly
detection.\cite{ATLAS2020,ATLAS2023,ATLAS2024,ATLAS2025,CMS2025}

Machine learning offers a natural framework for anomaly detection.
Approaches explored so far include
autoencoders,\cite{Hajer2020,Heimel2019,Farina2020,Cerri2019,Blance2019,Finke2021}
normalising flows and other density
estimators,\cite{Nachman2020,Andreassen2020,Hallin2022} and classification-based
methods.\cite{Metodiev2017,Collins2018,Collins2019,DAgnolo2019}
More recently, boosted decision trees have been shown to outperform deep
networks as classifiers in weakly supervised searches,\cite{Finke2024} and weak
supervision has been combined with information from a class of signal
models.\cite{Cheng2025}
The LHC Olympics (LHCO) 2020 challenge\cite{Kasieczka2021} provides
a standardised benchmark with a hidden dijet resonance signal, enabling
fair comparison across methods. The Dark Machines challenge\cite{Aarrestad2022}
offers a complementary event-level benchmark, and resonant weakly supervised methods
have been compared systematically on the LHCO R\&D dataset.\cite{Golling2024}

Despite this activity, four questions remain under-explored.
First, how sensitive are unsupervised detectors to accidental signal
contamination in the training set? Second, what does a
small set of labelled anomalies (weak supervision) add? Third, how much does each method
sculpt the dijet mass spectrum, and does a mass-decorrelated
architecture actually help? Fourth, do learned anomaly scores transfer
to structurally different signal topologies?

Here I address all four questions in a reproducible
study on the LHCO 2020 R\&D dataset. I reproduce and systematically
compare six anomaly-detection architectures, with controlled
randomisation over multiple seeds. I diagnose the collapse of Deep SVDD
and a label-assignment error in Deep SAD, and apply targeted fixes.
Mass sculpting and discrimination are characterised through the
Jensen--Shannon divergence (JSD), background rejection at fixed signal
efficiency, the maximum significance improvement characteristic and
bump-hunt significance, and generalisation is tested on an unseen
three-prong signal topology ($W' \to XY$, $X,Y \to qqq$). I additionally
implement a simple $\tautwo$ cut; resonant-search baselines (CWoLa Hunting, an
ANODE-style Gaussian-mixture density estimator, a CATHODE-style proxy and a tree-based
CWoLa variant); a mass-decorrelated sliced-Wasserstein detector with a semi-supervised
extension; and an extended seven-feature dataset that adds $\tauthree$ for both jets.
Follow-up studies test a constituent-level permutation-invariant model, an
independent external signal, sideband score calibration and background-only closure.

\section{Results}

\subhead{Benchmark overview}
I use the LHCO 2020 R\&D dataset,\cite{Kasieczka2021} which contains
1,000,000 simulated QCD dijet background events and 100,000 signal events
from $Z' \to XY$ with $X \to q\bar{q}$ and $Y \to q\bar{q}$. Each event is
described by five high-level jet-substructure features (\cref{tab:features} in Methods).
Thirteen detectors, M0--M12, span the supervision spectrum from purely heuristic cuts to
fully supervised classifiers (\cref{tab:models}). The neural models
share a bias-free linear-layer backbone, as the Deep SVDD family of
losses requires.\cite{Ruff2018,Ruff2020,Ruff2021} The headline comparison below concerns
M1--M6; the additional detectors M0 and M7--M12 are described in Methods, and
M7--M12 are compared in a dedicated subsection below.

\subhead{Failure modes}
\runin{Deep SVDD hypersphere collapse}
\textbf{Established in this benchmark:} Deep SVDD (M3) has near-random
nominal-signal ranking under the released implementation.\cite{Ruff2018}
\textbf{Plausible explanation:} low-variance embeddings and vanishing
gradients in a multilayer bias-free network could contribute to this
behaviour. The centre is initialised from training embeddings and its small
components are clamped away from zero; it is not fixed to a uniform vector.
No systematic centre/activation ablation record is included in this release.
The numerical result therefore does not establish a universal architectural
failure of Deep SVDD on tabular data.

\runin{Deep SAD label-assignment error}
The original training script incorrectly assigned $y=1$ (labelled
anomaly) to all signal events injected as unlabelled contamination,
rather than only to the $\klabels$ explicitly drawn labelled events.
This gave Deep SAD spurious positive labels and created an
artificial performance gain at $\ftrain > 0$, violating the
assumptions of semi-supervised learning. After the fix, unlabelled injected
events correctly receive $y=0$ (\cref{eq:sad}), and the contamination
benefit is attributed to the shift in the training-data distribution
rather than to falsely labelled anomalies.

\subhead{Mass-decorrelated and resonant-search baselines}
The remaining detectors in \cref{tab:headline} (M7--M12) address two of the questions posed in
the Introduction: whether explicit mass decorrelation helps, and how resonant-search
strategies compare with the substructure-based detectors.

The MD-SWAD implementations (M9 and M10) are exploratory extensions.
Their earlier numerical summaries are [unverified]: no matching multi-seed
evaluation table accompanies the saved checkpoints. They are excluded from
the final quantitative comparison. Explicit decorrelation is therefore a
candidate extension rather than an established result of this release.

The resonant-search baselines trail Isolation Forest (M2, $\auc = \IFMean$). ANODE with a Gaussian
mixture (M8) reaches $\auc = 0.720$, CWoLa Hunting (M7) $0.605$ and its tree-based variant
(M12) $0.575$, while the CATHODE (CVAE) proxy (M11) performs at chance level
($\auc = 0.481$, with $1/\eB = 1.2$ at every signal efficiency). M7, M8 and M12 also sculpt
the mass spectrum strongly ($\jsd = 0.189$, $0.058$ and $0.189$; $\rho = 0.433$, $0.337$ and
$0.450$), consistent with their use of $\mjj$-defined regions in training. Because M8, M11 and M12 are
simplified proxies of the published methods (Methods), these numbers characterise my
implementations rather than the methods themselves.

\subhead{Generalisation to an unseen signal topology}
I evaluate all headline models on the held-out three-prong signal sample
($X \to YY \to qqq$) to assess generalisation beyond the two-prong
training target. \Cref{fig:generalisation} shows the three-prong AUC against
the two-prong AUC for all seeds and headline configurations, and
\cref{tab:generalisation} gives the quantitative comparison.
The supervised MLP (M5) has the smallest generalisation gap
(see the measured gaps in the table), suggesting that the learned $\tautwo$ and jet-mass
discriminants partially transfer to the three-prong topology. Deep SAD
(M4) shows a larger gap, indicating that sparse
labelling is sufficient to learn partly topology-agnostic anomaly features.

\runin{Extended $\tau_{32}$ features}
\textbf{Plausible extension:} $\tauthree$ measures three-subjettiness relative
to two-subjettiness and may add information about three-prong substructure.\cite{Thaler2011}
The extended cache is available, but this release contains no matched
five-seed comparison establishing an AUC improvement from these inputs.

\subhead{Constituent-level representation and external signal}
To test an event representation beyond the five- and seven-feature inputs, I trained a
masked, permutation-invariant Deep Sets autoencoder on raw particle constituents. Each
event contains up to 700 $(p_T,\eta,\phi)$ triples, transformed into six channels:
$p_T/\sum p_T$, $\log(p_T/p_{T,\max})$, $\log(1+p_T)$, scaled $\eta$,
$\sin\phi$ and $\cos\phi$. The model was trained for eight epochs on a seeded subset
of 50,000 events from the 599,952-event background training partition; the best
validation-loss checkpoint was retained. A threshold for 1\% validation background
acceptance was fixed before testing. On the full original test pools, the two-prong and
three-prong AUCs were 0.498 and 0.441, with signal efficiencies of 0.160\% and 0.098\%
at that threshold. This run shows no useful separation from the constituent-level score.

I also evaluated all 44,673 events in the external $X\to YY'\to 4q$ sample
(2+2-prong topology) released with additional LHCO signal models.\cite{AdditionalSignals2026}
Against a seeded 20,000-event subset of held-out background, the AUC was 0.465 and the
signal efficiency at the frozen threshold was 0.159\%. This sample was not used in
training or threshold selection, and its generator chain differs from the original R\&D
benchmark. It is therefore reported as an external evaluation, not pooled with the
nominal benchmark.

The second external $W_{KK}\to WR\to3W$ sample has a 2+4-prong topology.
Its archive matches the published MD5 checksum; all 32,801 events were scored
with the same frozen checkpoint. Against the same seeded 20,000-event held-out
background subset, the AUC was 0.507 and the signal efficiency at the frozen
1\% threshold was 0.280\%. This near-random result is reported separately
because the generation chain differs from the original R\&D benchmark.

\subhead{Density-score calibration and background-only closure}
For the ANODE-style GMM, 128,112 held-out background events in the mass sidebands were
split into independent calibration and evaluation halves of 64,056 events each. I
converted the negative log-density score $s$ to an upper-tail conformal $p$-value,
$p=(1+\#\{s_i^{\rm cal}\geq s\})/(n_{\rm cal}+1)$. Under exchangeability of the
calibration and evaluation background, this gives finite-sample calibrated score
$p$-values.\cite{ShaferVovk2008} The evaluation values were consistent with uniformity
(KS statistic 0.0043, $p=0.179$); observed rates below 1\%, 5\% and 10\% were 1.054\%,
4.975\% and 9.868\%. This calibrates the anomaly-score tail for sideband background,
not an absolute density or a signal-region conditional density. Consistent with this
distinction, the raw GMM Rosenblatt-PIT marginals remained nonuniform on the evaluation
half (KS statistics 0.026--0.052, all $p<10^{-38}$).

As a background-only closure check, I generated 500 Poisson-bootstrap pseudoexperiments
from held-out background, froze each detector threshold using validation background,
and repeated the local sideband fit. At the 1\% operating point, the 95th-percentile
local significances were 1.86 (M1), 1.19 (M2) and 1.51 (M11); the fractions with
$Z\geq3$ were 0/461, 0/479 and 1/456 among valid fits. These are local conditional
closure results for the released simulation and chosen fit, not global significance
estimates or detector-systematic closure.

\section{Discussion}

I have presented a benchmark of unsupervised and semi-supervised
anomaly detection on the LHCO 2020 R\&D dataset. Weak supervision is
highly label-efficient: with $\klabels = 100$ labelled anomalies, Deep SAD
achieves $\auc = \SADMean$, recovering much of the AUC gap between the best
unsupervised baseline (Isolation Forest, $\auc = \IFHeadline$) and the
supervised reference ($\auc = \SupMean$). Its fixed-window local diagnostics on
the simulated signal mixture are reported separately from its measured
mass sculpting ($\jsd = \SADJSD$). Isolation Forest is the best learned
unsupervised detector on this five-dimensional feature set and is robust
to training contamination. Deep SVDD, by contrast, collapses on zero-centred
tabular data in this implementation. The mechanism remains uncertain and
the limited implementation comparison warrants caution when
applying SVDD to standardised tabular data in high-energy physics. Generalisation
to an unseen three-prong topology is partial but meaningful: Deep SAD
retains $\auc = \SADTransferMean$ without any three-prong training events.

These results offer practical guidance, though they must be interpreted within the
scope of a simulation-only benchmark. The headline comparisons use low-dimensional
features and a nominal signal; follow-up tests add one external signal and a
constituent-level model but do not establish broad signal-model coverage. Within the
headline regime,
Isolation Forest is a sensible unsupervised baseline: it
needs no signal assumption, trains in minutes and is robust to contamination
up to $1\%$. Deep SAD should be preferred over Deep SVDD for similar tabular feature sets,
because it performs better in this measured setup; this is not a claim that
the repulsion term removes all degenerate fixed points.
For the two-prong signal studied here, 100 labelled examples improve on the
learned unsupervised baseline, while the seed-42 grid shows additional gains
with 1,000 labels. A repeated-seed grid would be needed to establish the
trade-off between label cost and performance.

Mass sculpting must be evaluated carefully before any score cut is deployed. Deep SAD and
Isolation Forest showed acceptable JSD ($< 0.03$) at $10\%$ background efficiency in this
five-feature space, but the autoencoder and mass-aware SVDD would require additional
decorrelation in any production search, such as distance correlation\cite{Kasieczka2020}
or adversarial training.\cite{Louppe2017,Shimmin2017} The exploratory MD-SWAD
implementations require a verified repeated-seed evaluation before their
discrimination--decorrelation trade-off can be quantified.
Resonant methods designed to avoid background sculpting are discussed in
Ref.~\citenum{Hallin2023}. The seven-feature $\tauthree$ dataset remains a
candidate for direct high-level model evaluation. The separate constituent-level
Deep Sets test reported here did not improve discrimination, showing that higher
input dimensionality alone is insufficient.

In principle, CWoLa Hunting is complementary to the substructure-based scores, because it uses the
$\mjj$ window as a physics-motivated weak label. In this study, however, the simplified
resonant-search baselines (M7, M8, M11 and M12) all trailed Isolation Forest and, for M7, M8 and M12,
sculpted the mass spectrum strongly. They should be revisited with full implementations,
such as normalising-flow density estimators, before any conclusion is drawn about the published methods.
The original M0--M12 comparison uses five- or seven-feature inputs; the follow-up
Deep Sets model instead processes up to 700 constituents with six channels per
constituent, but its AUCs remain near or below chance. These results provide a
low-level reference point alongside the high-level baselines for future collider
anomaly-detection studies, including emerging quantum approaches.\cite{Ngairangbam2022,Alvi2023}

\section{Methods}

\runin{Dataset}
The LHCO 2020 R\&D dataset\cite{Kasieczka2021,Kasieczka2019data} consists of
1,100,000 dijet events: 1,000,000 QCD background events produced with
Pythia\,8\cite{Sjostrand2015} and Delphes,\cite{deFavereau2014} and
100,000 signal events from the process $W' \to XY$,
$X \to q\bar{q}$ and $Y \to q\bar{q}$, at $m_{W'} = 3.5$~TeV,
$m_X = 500$~GeV and $m_Y = 100$~GeV. The signal produces a
characteristic two-pronged topology in each of the two leading jets.
A second, held-out signal sample with a three-prong topology
($W' \to XY$, $X,Y \to qqq$, with the same particle masses) is used exclusively for
the generalisation evaluation. Events are split into disjoint training,
validation and test sets (\cref{tab:splits}). Training-set signal events
are used only when explicitly injected (contamination studies) or
labelled (Deep SAD and supervised models).

\runin{Feature extraction}
High-level features are derived from the two leading anti-$k_T$ jets\cite{Cacciari2008}

implementation.\cite{Thaler2011} The five standard features used by
all models are listed in \cref{tab:features}. The dijet invariant mass
$\mjj$ is deliberately withheld from the standard feature set to prevent
trivial mass sculpting; it is used only by mass-aware SVDD (M6), CWoLa (M7, M12), ANODE (M8), MD-SWAD (M9, M10), and CATHODE (M11). All features are standardised (zero mean, unit variance)
with a \code{StandardScaler}\cite{Pedregosa2011} fitted on the background training set only,
never on signal events.

\runin{Extended feature set}
For the generalisation study, I additionally compute the 3-to-2
N-subjettiness ratio $\tauthree^{J_{1,2}} = \tau_3/\tau_2$ for both jets,
extending the feature vector to seven dimensions. This variable directly
discriminates three-prong decay products from QCD dijets. The extended dataset is
stored at \code{data/processed/events\_v2\_extended\_features.h5}.

\runin{Raw-particle extension}
The separate constituent-level study reads up to 700 raw $(p_T,\eta,\phi)$ triples
per event and maps each particle to six channels: $p_T/\sum p_T$,
$\log(p_T/p_{T,\max})$, $\log(1+p_T)$, scaled $\eta$, $\sin\phi$ and
$\cos\phi$. A shared particle encoder is followed by masked mean and maximum
pooling, an event embedding, and a per-particle decoder. The event anomaly score is
the mean squared reconstruction error over valid particles; it is a ranking score,
not a likelihood. Training uses background only: 50,000 seeded events from a
599,952-event training partition for eight epochs. The checkpoint with the lowest
validation loss is kept, and the score threshold is fixed at the validation 99th
percentile with randomized acceptance at tied scores. All original test pools are
scored. The additional $X\to YY'\to4q$ sample contains 44,673 events and comes from
Zenodo record 18983506;\cite{AdditionalSignals2026} its AUC is measured against a
seeded 20,000-event subset of held-out background. The external
$W_{KK}\to WR\to3W$ sample from the same record contains 32,801 events and is
evaluated with the same checkpoint, background subset and threshold.

\runin{Conformal density-score calibration}
For the ANODE-style GMM, held-out background events in the 2.5--3.3 and
3.7--4.5~TeV sidebands are divided into disjoint calibration and evaluation halves.
For evaluation score $s$, the upper-tail conformal $p$-value is
$(1+\#\{s_i^{\rm cal}\geq s\})/(n_{\rm cal}+1)$, with ties counted
conservatively. Its finite-sample validity requires exchangeability between these
two background halves.\cite{ShaferVovk2008} The resulting values calibrate the score
tail for sideband background only; they are not an absolute density estimate and
do not establish transfer to the signal region.

\runin{Background-only closure}
For M1, M2 and M11, validation-background thresholds are frozen before generating
500 Poisson-bootstrap pseudoexperiments from held-out background. Score ties at the
cut use randomized boundary acceptance. The local sideband fit is repeated for each
trial; failed or saturated fits are excluded, so the valid count is reported for
each model and working point. This conditional simulation closure does not include
detector systematics, alternative background generators or a global search.

\runin{Model architectures}
SVDD and SAD use bias-free layers with architecture
$5 \to 64 \to 32 \to 16$. The autoencoder has an encoder
$5 \to 64 \to 32 \to 16$ and a symmetric decoder, with biases and
LeakyReLU activations. The supervised reference uses biased layers
$5 \to 64 \to 32 \to 1$ with ReLU activations. The mass-aware model
has six inputs; the hidden widths are otherwise unchanged.

\runin{Loss functions}
The autoencoder (M1) minimises the mean-squared reconstruction error,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AE}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i - \hat{x}_i\|^2 .
\end{equation}
Deep SVDD\cite{Ruff2018} (M3) minimises the mean squared distance from a
fixed centre $c$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SVDD}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \|\phi(x_i) - c\|^2 .
\end{equation}
Deep SAD\cite{Ruff2020} (M4) extends this with an inverse-distance
penalty for labelled anomalies ($y_i = 1$),
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SAD}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{y_i=0}\|\phi(x_i)-c\|^2
  + \frac{\eta}{n}\sum_{y_i=1}\left(\|\phi(x_i)-c\|^2\right)^{-1},
\label{eq:sad}
\end{equation}
where $\eta > 0$ controls the strength of the anomaly repulsion. Unlabelled
events ($y_i = 0$) include both background training events and any
signal events injected without labels.
To resolve the Deep SVDD collapse and explicitly decorrelate the latent representation from the dijet mass $\mjj$, I propose the Mass-Decorrelated Sliced Wasserstein Anomaly Detector (MD-SWAD, M9). MD-SWAD maps the background directly onto a standard normal target distribution using the sliced Wasserstein distance (SWD),\cite{Bonneel2015,Kolouri2019} and adds a distance-correlation (DisCo) penalty\cite{Kasieczka2020,Szekely2007}, weighted by $\lambda$, that decorrelates the embedding from $\mjj$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MD-SWAD}} = \mathrm{SWD}(\phi(X_{y=0}), \mathcal{N}(0, I)) + \lambda \cdot \mathrm{dCor}^2(\phi(X_{y=0}), \mjj).
\end{equation}
For the semi-supervised variant (MD-SWAD-SAD, M10), the Deep SAD repulsion term for the labelled anomalies ($y_i = 1$) is added to push them away from the origin:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MD-SWAD-SAD}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MD-SWAD}} + \frac{\eta}{n}\sum_{y_i=1}\left(\|\phi(x_i)\|^2\right)^{-1}.
\end{equation}

\runin{Training protocol}
All neural models are implemented in PyTorch\cite{Paszke2019} and trained with Adam\cite{Kingma2015}
(learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-5}$), a batch size of 256 and
early stopping with patience 8 on the validation loss. The checkpoint
with the lowest validation objective is selected. Five seeds
(42--46) are used for the six core headline comparisons;
supplemental baselines are single-seed exploratory evaluations.
The final core results use 47 fresh fits in an isolated Python 3.11 CPU environment;
dependency versions and the staged 33-minute verification are recorded in the repository.
Historical neural results differ and are preserved separately. For the final seed-42
detectors, 500 stratified paired-bootstrap trials on 199,984 background and 19,996
signal test events provide separate AUC intervals in the released machine-readable
tables. These finite-test intervals are conditional on the fitted models and are
not combined with training-seed standard deviations.

\runin{Contamination and label budget}
The training contamination $\ftrain$ is the number of unlabelled signal
events injected into the training set, expressed as a fraction of the
background training pool size ($N_\mathrm{bkg}=599{,}952$). For example,
$\ftrain = 0.5\%$ adds $2{,}999$ signal events to the training set without
labels. The label budget $\klabels$ (Deep SAD only) is the number of distinct signal
events assigned a positive label ($y=1$) from the remaining training-signal pool. These
events are drawn after the contamination injection and are
mutually exclusive with the unlabelled injected events.

\runin{Evaluation metrics}
I report the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC)
together with the background rejection $1/\eB$ at signal
efficiencies $\eS \in \{10\%, 30\%, 50\%\}$. The maximum significance
improvement characteristic is
\begin{equation}
\sic_\mathrm{max} = \max_t \frac{\eS(t)}{\sqrt{\eB(t)}} .
\end{equation}
Mass sculpting is quantified by the Jensen--Shannon divergence
\begin{equation}
\jsd(P \| Q) = \frac{1}{2} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|M) + \frac{1}{2} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|M),
\end{equation}
where $M = (P+Q)/2$, $P$ is the normalised inclusive background
$\mjj$ distribution and $Q$ is the normalised $\mjj$ distribution
after a score cut at a fixed background efficiency; $\jsd = 0$ means
no sculpting. I also report the Pearson correlation coefficient
$\rho(s, \mjj)$ between anomaly scores $s$ and $\mjj$ on the background
test sample, for which $|\rho| \to 0$ is ideal. The bump-hunt significance is
the local excess statistic $Z$ with propagated background-fit uncertainty.
The background expectation comes from a binned Poisson fit to sidebands
in 2.5--4.5~TeV, excluding the fixed 3.3--3.7~TeV window. The log-background
model uses the basis $1$, $\log(1-x)$, $-\log x$ and $-(\log x)^2$, with
$x=\mjj/(13~\mathrm{TeV})$. No Gaussian signal template is fitted.
Definitions of all metrics are
collected in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:metrics}.

\runin{Additional baselines}
CWoLa Hunting\cite{Metodiev2017,Collins2018,Collins2019} (M7; Classification Without Labels) uses the
$\mjj$ distribution to define a signal region (SR: $3300$--$3700$~GeV)
and sidebands (SB: $2500$--$3300$ and $3700$--$4500$~GeV). A
classifier trained to separate SR from SB events learns to isolate signal
(preferentially in the SR) from background, using $\mjj$ as a
surrogate label rather than a direct anomaly tag. The approach is
model-agnostic and requires no signal simulation. My implementation
uses a compact MLP ($5 \to 64 \to 64 \to 1$, \code{ReLU} activations,
\code{BCEWithLogitsLoss}) trained only on events within the SR+SB window,
and is activated by the configuration flag \code{use\_mjj: true} in
\code{m7\_cwola.yaml}. ANODE\cite{Nachman2020} (M8) estimates the
background density from sideband events and scores anomalies by the
likelihood ratio $p_{\mathrm{SR}}(x)/p_{\mathrm{SB}}(x)$. My implementation
approximates the sideband density with a five-component Gaussian mixture
model (GMM) fitted on $x \in \mathrm{SB}$ and uses the score
$-\log p_{\mathrm{SB}}(x)$ as a proxy for the likelihood ratio. A full ANODE
implementation with normalising flows\cite{Papamakarios2021} would give a
more accurate density estimate, particularly near the SR boundary.

CATHODE\cite{Hallin2022} (M11) normally replaces the sideband density model with flow-based
sampling of the background in the signal region, and then trains a classifier to distinguish
data from the synthetic background. Because a full implementation requires a normalising flow, I
implemented a computationally tractable proxy, CATHODE (CVAE), in which a conditional variational
autoencoder\cite{Sohn2015,KingmaWelling2014} generates the pseudo-background in place of the flow.
Finally, CWoLa Trees (M12) follows the same weak-supervision methodology as M7 but replaces the
neural network with a histogram-based gradient-boosting classifier,\cite{Pedregosa2011} a standard
tree-based weakly supervised baseline.\cite{Finke2024}
The $\tautwo$-cut baseline (M0) scores events by $-(\tautwo^{J_1}+\tautwo^{J_2})$.
